The Article

Why This Philosophy Department Is Banning AI

By Shea Vance, The Chronicle of Higher Education, 17 November 2026 • A Q&A with Shane Ewegen, professor of philosophy at Trinity College, Hartford, Conn. • Interview edited for length and clarity in the original.

THE DECISION

Trinity College’s philosophy department has barred students from using generative AI on coursework, arguing that the technology undermines the department’s mission of teaching students “to think, to read, to write, to speak, to reason, and to live ethically.” Blanket departmental bans like this are still rare: most academic units leave AI policy to individual professors.

Ewegen and his colleagues began deliberating in the spring, exchanged emails over the summer, agreed unanimously in September on a concise three-sentence rule, and notified the Hartford campus community in October. The rule forbids any student use of AI “in any capacity and for any purpose” in philosophy courses — specifically including brainstorming, writing or editing, and constructing bibliographies, whether in preparation for class or in the classroom.

WHY ONE RULE FOR THE WHOLE DEPARTMENT?

Ewegen, speaking for his own perspective — he notes colleagues were unanimous on the policy and its language but may reason about it differently — says the talks started with confusion. If each professor allowed different uses, students would face a patchwork. A single, flat standard would at least simplify expectations for philosophy students.

As the faculty talked, they found they were largely in agreement: all were against academic uses of AI, emphatically and in any capacity. Early drafts ranged from long and detailed to very short; they settled on the short version.

WHAT ABOUT THE COLLEGE’S OWN GUIDANCE?

Ewegen says students were also hearing a different message from the administration. Trinity’s collegewide guidance was devised, in his account, with minimal faculty input and no faculty vote. It makes faculty responsible for defining course goals, communicating expectations, and evaluating fairly, and it frames students as making choices about their own learning — including integrity, self-reflection, and whether AI will replace their own thinking — as preparation for a world where AI is present.

A Trinity spokesperson told The Chronicle that college leaders recognize AI’s rapid expansion, remain committed to open dialogue, and follow the common practice of letting faculty decide when and how AI is used in their courses based on expertise and learning objectives — while also believing students should be prepared to use these tools responsibly and effectively.

WHY NOT ALLOW SOME USE, WITH DISCLOSURE?

The Chronicle notes that other units have taken different routes — the University of Chicago restricting AI and tech in class in its social-sciences division, Duke’s classical-studies department expressly prohibiting AI on assignments, and still others allowing moderate use if students are transparent about it. Asked why a ban is better than allowed use with disclosure, Ewegen says the department had seen no evidence that using AI to brainstorm, edit, write, or act as a study interlocutor for oral finals promotes its learning goals. To the contrary, he cites more and more reports of harmful effects on students’ abilities to think, read, and write, and says the faculty could not conceive of a use that facilitated understanding philosophical argument, developing original theses, or defending them in writing or orally.

He adds an ethical argument that would stand even if a use helped learning: the environmental impact of developing and using AI, and mineral mining in the Global South for AI chips contaminating water supplies. On that basis, he describes the technology at this stage as not redeemable for the philosophical classroom.

THE STICKING POINT: PUNISHMENT

The policy as adopted lists no punitive repercussions. That was deliberate. Ewegen wanted a sentence stating that a student caught using AI would fail the class; colleagues pointed out that professors might reasonably want different outcomes in different cases. Consequences were left to each professor’s discretion. In Ewegen’s own practice, substantive AI use usually means failing the assignment, and more likely the course.

WHAT HE HOPES HAPPENS NEXT

Ewegen, who earlier spearheaded a faculty effort calling on Trinity to divest from AI companies, hopes the ban provokes other departments to codify and publish policies of their own, broadens conversation about the technology’s dangers, and pushes the administration — whose outward stance he describes as a pretty-positive endorsement of AI — toward a more critical position.

CAN A BAN LIKE THIS ACTUALLY WORK?

Ewegen concedes enforcement is hard because AI is sophisticated and ubiquitous, and that policing every step would make the classroom unpleasant for everyone. His alternative is open, honest conversation at the start of the semester about why the technology is harmful for philosophy, so students see concern rather than mere policing, and then trust. He has also stopped assigning papers written outside class: all writing in his courses is now done in class, by hand — a change he calls an enormous loss, because writing is not incidental to philosophical thinking but essential to it, and the struggle of learning to write is the struggle of learning to think.

Finally, he rejects the argument that AI is inevitable and students should simply be taught to work with it. In his view, corporations have marketed AI as the next inevitable stage of progress; technologies spread because people choose to use them, markets respond to refusal and financial pressure, and institutions that spend heavily on AI suites could demand more ethical versions. Slowing the technology down, he argues, will require exactly this kind of public concern and policy.

Discussion prompt: validate the department

Your post (about 250-350 words). Pick the department’s central claim — any use of generative AI, in any capacity, undermines philosophy learning, so a blanket ban is justified — and run it through all five steps. Do not just give an opinion: show your evidence for each step.

  1. Track the source
    Who is actually speaking, and on what evidence? Where does Ewegen speak for himself vs. the whole department? What sources, if any, are named for the claim that AI harms thinking, reading, and writing?
  2. Check the dates
    Is the evidence current for the AI tools students have today? Could a judgment formed earlier in this debate still hold, or has the technology - or the research - moved?
  3. Corroborate the story
    Find one second, non-AI source that confirms or contradicts a key department claim - for example on learning effects, environmental impact, or how other colleges handle this. Name it and say: matches, or contradicts?
  4. Scan for omissions / bias
    Whose perspective is left out - students, the administration, other kinds of AI use? What is the strongest argument for the other side that the article never answers?
  5. Verify one detail exactly
    Pick one specific claim - the three-sentence rule, the timeline, the enforcement change to handwritten in-class writing, or another - and verify it against the article or your second source. Quote the detail you checked.

End With a Verdict and a Correction

  • Admissible - the ban is justified as stated
  • Inadmissible - it is not justified as stated
  • Partly admissible - say exactly which part holds

Then name one specific correction: what should the policy say instead - a narrower ban, an allowed use with disclosure, a different consequence - and why your evidence supports it. Finish with your AI-use line above.

Then Reply to One Classmate (Required, About 100-150 Words)

  • Name one Evidence Protocol step in their post and engage with it specifically - add a source, challenge their corroboration, or supply the perspective they missed.
  • End with one genuine question their verdict raises for you.
  • “I agree, great post” alone does not count as a reply.
